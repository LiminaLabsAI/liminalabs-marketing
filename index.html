Three questions

Most teams can answer one of these. Very few can answer all three.

Can you say what the AI was supposed to do — and who agreed to it?

Work reaches people and agents as a sentence, not a specification. Everyone fills the gaps with assumptions, and they stack silently until the output is wrong. Agents rarely fail because the model is weak — they fail because the intent was thin.

Can you stop it doing something else — and prove what it did?

When limits live inside prompts, the model can be argued past them, and data leaves through third-party APIs. When the auditor asks what happened, there needs to be an answer that holds up.

Does what you learn survive the next project?

The reasoning happened inside a model call and then vanished. The next team starts from zero, and when a senior person leaves their judgement leaves too.

Where the answer is no, the cost is quiet and compounding: every new use case becomes another AI engineering project, and nothing accumulates. Intelligence gets rented and rebuilt, quarter after quarter.

If you can answer all three today, you don't need us.

The insight

Sovereignty is not about hosting weights. It is about owning your reasoning.

Models are the muscle — rented, replaceable, changing under you. What must not be interchangeable is the mind: what your organisation is trying to do, what it is allowed to do, and what it has learned. Today that lives in people's heads, in brittle prompts, and inside vendor runtimes. So it is not really owned at all.
