What it is

A layer the enterprise owns, sitting between it and whichever AI models it uses — where work is specified before it starts, execution is governed while it runs, and what was learned is kept afterwards.

Who it is for

Enterprises in high-precision, regulated environments — finance, healthcare, legal, regulated manufacturing — and the executive who owns the risk when an agent touches a live process.

What it does

Turns a thin instruction into a written intent people agreed to, runs it inside limits the model cannot argue its way past, and keeps the trace as evidence.

Why it matters

You can widen what your agents decide for themselves and still defend having let them — afterwards, to someone who is not in a good mood.

The problem

Three failures, and every enterprise has all three at once.

First failure

Intent is thin.

Work gets handed to people and agents as a sentence, not a specification. Everyone fills the gaps with assumptions, and the assumptions stack silently until the output is wrong.

Agents do not fail because the model is weak. They fail because the intent was thin.

Second failure

Execution is ungoverned.

Data leaves through third-party APIs. Every team applies different rules. Limits live inside prompts, where the model can talk its way past them.

When the auditor asks what happened, there is no answer that holds up.

Third failure

Learning evaporates.

The reasoning happened inside a model call and then vanished. The next team starts from zero. When the senior person leaves, their judgement leaves too.

And when the provider ships an update, the prompts that encoded your rules quietly break.

The compounding cost Every new use case becomes another AI engineering project. Nothing accumulates. You are renting intelligence and rebuilding it every quarter.
Capability is purchasable. Permission is not.

Any enterprise can buy frontier reasoning this afternoon. What it cannot buy is the ability to defend having let that reasoning act.

The insight

Sovereignty is not about hosting weights. It is about owning your reasoning.

Models are becoming a commodity. They are interchangeable, they get cheaper, and they will keep changing under you. That is fine — that is the muscle. What must not be interchangeable is the mind.

What must be yours

What you are trying to do, what you are allowed to do, and what you have learned.

Today that lives in people's heads, in brittle prompts, and inside vendor runtimes. So it is not really owned at all.

We make that layer explicit, governed and portable — and put it on the enterprise's side of the boundary.

Why the boundary sits outside the model

You cannot make a system safe by asking it nicely.

Prompts and fine-tuning are requests, not guarantees — the model can be argued out of them. A real limit has to sit outside the model, where the model cannot reach it.

Formally: perfect alignment of a sufficiently expressive model's internal weights is not merely difficult, it is uncomputable — for the same reason the halting problem and Gödel's incompleteness results hold. More compute does not close the gap. The engineering response is not to solve the unsolvable but to encapsulate it: wrap the probabilistic system in a deterministic boundary that enforces limits it cannot alter.

Therefore That boundary is Precepta. The theory and the product are the same thing.

The instrument

Every vendor picks a point on the autonomy spectrum and hard-codes it.

Copilots stay propose-only — safe, and permanently limited, with a human as the throughput ceiling forever. Autonomous agents act by default — impressive in a demo, unsellable into a regulated process, and they ask you to accept the whole risk in a single leap. Autonomy is not a property of a product. It is a decision an enterprise makes per domain and per risk class, and changes its mind about.

The stack

Three products. Layers, not stages.

The control loop runs inside Intent Studio, on top of Precepta, grounded by Cerebrio. Nothing is compulsory: each product works on its own, inside someone else's stack, with neither of the others present. They are independent layers and composable.

The desired state

Intent Studio↗

Governed work for people and their agents.

Public · early access

A workspace where a team and its agents converge on one written intent before work starts. An intent is one real piece of work — a line changeover, a migration, an audit response — held as a structured record instead of a thread.

Every line in it is a claim with a state: proposed, agreed, contested or escalated, attributed by name. Disagreement stays on the record instead of being averaged away, and an agent cannot promote its own claim.

The autonomy dial above lives here, and so do the evaluation criteria for an intent. It is not staying a specification tool: the direction is that Intent Studio generates the spec artifacts and carries out the execution itself, with its own coding, research and other agents.

The idea nobody else prices

Slop is unnecessary or unjustified autonomous activity.

Not "too many tokens" — wasted autonomy. Work an agent did that nobody needed. Everyone measures whether an agent succeeded. Almost nobody measures whether it should have done that much work at all.

Maximum intent progress, with minimum unnecessary autonomous activity, inside limits the system cannot trade away.

Making slop a first-class, evaluated, optimised quantity is the most differentiated idea in the platform. Risk is a constraint, not a penalty — it is not something the optimiser is allowed to trade away.

Why it holds

The record appreciates while the models depreciate.

00 · the moat

The outer loop is the moat.

Everyone optimises the inner loop — get this task done. Only a system that keeps traces, evaluations and reflection can run the outer loop, where the way of working itself improves. That is what compounds, and it cannot be retrofitted onto a stateless agent stack.

01

Anyone can rent the same model. Nobody else has your accumulated intents, evidence, decisions and outcomes. That asset appreciates while the model layer depreciates.

02

Switching cost sits on the right side.

Because it is model-agnostic, you can leave any model vendor cheaply. Leaving us means leaving the record of how your organisation actually works.

03

Reuse.

Once an intent is specified and validated in one plant, team or business unit, redeploying it costs close to nothing. Margin scales with reuse.

04

Governance is a moat in regulated markets.

Attestation and tamper-evident audit are not features you add later; they are architecture. Finance, healthcare and legal cannot buy the ungoverned version at any price.

The usual questions

What people ask in the first meeting.

How is this different from RAG?

RAG fetches text to answer a question. We govern a piece of work: what was intended, what was permitted, what happened, what was learned. Retrieval is a step; this is the loop around it.

Isn't this just guardrails?

Guardrails are usually prompt instructions, and the model can be argued past them. Ours are external and deterministic — enforced outside the model, where it cannot reach them.

Why not build it on the model vendor's platform?

Then your rules live in their runtime, and you rebuild when they change it. We put that layer on your side of the boundary, so changing model vendor costs you nothing.

Does anything leave our network?

In the self-hosted deployment, no. It runs against your models, your identity provider and your audit store, licensed by key with no telemetry and no phone-home. Zero-egress is verified by live probes and backed by a signed attestation.

Do we have to adopt all three products?

No. Each is independently sellable and works on its own, inside someone else's stack. Buy Precepta for sovereign model infrastructure, or Intent Studio for governed agentic work. Most organisations start with one and compose later.

You're early and unfunded. Why you?

Because this has to be architecture, not a feature. Incumbents built for a world where the model was trusted. We started from the assumption that it cannot be.
